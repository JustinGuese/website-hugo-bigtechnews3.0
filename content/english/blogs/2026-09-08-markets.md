<h2 id="apples-foldable-launches-into-occupied-ground">Apple&#x27;s foldable launches into occupied ground</h2>

Apple's long-expected foldable will arrive roughly two days after its chief Chinese rival's second-generation tri-fold - a 10.2-inch display, a battery north of 6,000mAh, and a second iteration of a form factor Apple hasn't shipped once. The expected Apple price is above $2,000, while a cheaper domestic foldable sits underneath it.

The second-order point isn't the spec sheet. It's that the foldable was framed as the premium hardware event that reignites the upgrade cycle, and in China - the market where premium hardware still has real pricing power - the top of that price band is now contested by a rival on its second generation and undercut from below. A first-gen product arriving late, at the highest price in the category, into a tier someone else has spent two cycles establishing, is a margin story before it's a unit story. Nothing here changes the demand picture overnight, but it removes the "uncontested premium launch" bull case. We stay negative, at low conviction, consistent with yesterday.

<h2 id="the-first-robot-production-line---and-whose-chips-are-in-it">The first robot production line - and whose chips are in it</h2>

A Chinese EV maker started what it calls the world's first automated production line for humanoid robots: a 76-degree-of-freedom machine, mass production targeted by end-2026, and - the detail that matters to us - three in-house Turing-class chips delivering 2,250 TOPS of onboard compute.

The obvious read is "robots are scaling." The second-order read is about the silicon. Every serious entrant is verticalizing the robot brain: Tesla runs its own inference chip, and now the first mover on automated production is doing the same. The robotics bull case for merchant accelerators keeps quietly shrinking - the edge goes in-house, and what's left for the merchant market is the training clusters behind these programs. That's a narrative risk for the "robots will buy accelerators by the million" thesis more than a revenue hit today, but it's worth marking. Note also the timeline: end-2026 mass production means physical-AI capex shows up in 2027 supplier revenue, not this quarter.

The rare-earth-free motor matters to the magnet supply chain, not to any name we follow. Neutral, unchanged.

<h2 id="video-generation-gets-a-third-entrant">Video generation gets a third entrant</h2>

The company behind the world's largest short-video app is reportedly close to launching a real-time spatial video generation model, competing directly with Meta and Alphabet.

The word that matters is *real-time*. Batch video generation is a demo; real-time generation is a persistent inference workload that runs whenever users touch it. If three of the largest compute buyers on the planet are now racing to serve generated video at interactive latency - one of them with the distribution to put it in front of a billion-plus users on day one - that is structural inference demand layered on top of an already tight compute picture. The beneficiary is not the video platforms, which will spend to compete; it's whoever sells the accelerators underneath. We add a low-conviction positive on that basis, not on any single contract.
