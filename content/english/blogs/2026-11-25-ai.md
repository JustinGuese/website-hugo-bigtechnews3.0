<p class="small mb-4"><a href="https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/x3R9ZaCzkfmsnCvuHk7usg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8dnAtLXZucC10aGUtcGVybWFuZW50LWlzLWhhcmQtYW5kLXRoYXQtbWF0dGVyc3x8aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZnJpZWRnaWwuY29tL3Zwdm5wP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Source: friedgil.com ↗</a></p>

[Anthropic's proof](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/x3R9ZaCzkfmsnCvuHk7usg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8dnAtLXZucC10aGUtcGVybWFuZW50LWlzLWhhcmQtYW5kLXRoYXQtbWF0dGVyc3x8aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZnJpZWRnaWwuY29tL3Zwdm5wP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) shows that computing the permanent of a matrix cannot be done in polynomial time using arithmetic circuits of polynomial size. This is the VP ≠ VNP problem, and it has a direct implication for P vs NP: for P to equal NP, there must be a trick using Boolean operators (AND/OR/NOT) that beats addition and multiplication for permanents.

The permanent is #P-complete, so a polynomial-time method would be huge. The proof doesn't settle P vs NP - it shows that arithmetic circuits alone can't do it, and there are problems where Boolean operators genuinely help. But it narrows the field considerably. There's a $1 million prize for a solution to the broader problem, and this is the kind of result that makes theoretical CS people very excited and everyone else wonder why they should care. The short answer: if you ever rely on matrix operations being tractable, this is foundational.

<p class="small mb-4"><a href="https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/ebj_10sxSosSsuKyuz2aFw/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8c3dhcm1zLWFyZS1mYXN0ZXItbm90LXNtYXJ0ZXJ8fGh0dHBzOi8vd3d3LnBhcmFkaWdtMy5vcmcvcmVzZWFyY2gvc3dhcm1zP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Source: paradigm3.org ↗</a></p>

[A study from Paradigm3](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/ebj_10sxSosSsuKyuz2aFw/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8c3dhcm1zLWFyZS1mYXN0ZXItbm90LXNtYXJ0ZXJ8fGh0dHBzOi8vd3d3LnBhcmFkaWdtMy5vcmcvcmVzZWFyY2gvc3dhcm1zP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) compared LLM "swarms" - teams of 2, 4, or 8 agents - against a single agent with the same total token budget across over 1,100 runs. Swarms were 6x faster but suffered a ~7% quality penalty, and the penalty grows with swarm size. Teams of 16 and 32 agents did no better than 8.

The key finding: a single agent that can restart its context beats self-organized teams. Previous "swarm premiums" were actually due to stale contexts - a single agent accumulates garbage over a long run, while swarms get fresh context but lose coherence. The authors estimate a "stepping on each other's toes" parameter of 0.6-0.9, concluding swarms are not a dominant strategy or scaling axis. If you're building multi-agent systems, this is the evidence that you're paying for parallelism, not capability.

[vLLM-GR is an open-source inference and serving framework](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/2WF9BwcYyL4zXNkIF_wWJg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8dmxsbS1nci1icmluZ3MtYmVhbS1zZWFyY2gtdG8tcmVjb21tZW5kYXRpb258fGh0dHBzOi8vdmxsbS5haS9ibG9nLzIwMjYtMTEtMjQtdmxsbS1nci1hbm5vdW5jZW1lbnQ_dXRtX3NvdXJjZT1icmllZi10ZWNoLW5ld3MuY29t) built on vLLM for generative recommendation models. <p class="small mb-4"><a href="https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/tXEPW3d7OdCQIHIF_41jfw/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8bWwtY29tcGlsZXItbGF5b3V0LXNlbGVjdGlvbi1pcy1ucC1oYXJkfHxodHRwczovL2lhbmJhcmJlci5ibG9nLzIwMjYvMTEvMjQvbWwtY29tcGlsZXItZW5nLXlvdXItam9iLWlzLW5wLWhhcmQvP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Source: ianbarber.blog ↗</a></p>

[A paper from Amazon shows layout selection in ML compilers is NP-hard](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/tXEPW3d7OdCQIHIF_41jfw/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtYWl8bWwtY29tcGlsZXItbGF5b3V0LXNlbGVjdGlvbi1pcy1ucC1oYXJkfHxodHRwczovL2lhbmJhcmJlci5ibG9nLzIwMjYvMTEvMjQvbWwtY29tcGlsZXItZW5nLXlvdXItam9iLWlzLW5wLWhhcmQvP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ), even with constant inputs and small layout counts. On ResNet-50, an exact solver takes 63.9M cycles versus 20.9M for heuristic rules - a 3x gap attributed to a wonky cost model.

The author's experiments show Triton's RemoveLayoutConversions heuristic overestimates conversion costs by ~4x for fp32 and ~19x for fp16. But updating the cost model doesn't change most kernels, and rematerialization often avoids conversions. The default layout picks are mostly fine. The takeaway: your compiler's heuristics are doing a decent job, but there's real headroom if you can afford the search.
