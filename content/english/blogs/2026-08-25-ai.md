NVIDIA just put Groq 3 LPX into full production. The chip is an inference accelerator that bolts onto Vera Rubin racks, and NVIDIA is claiming it delivers the fastest token generation ever recorded: 3,400 tokens per second on a Vera Rubin NVL72, with a 4x response-time edge over what it calls the nearest alternative. Tasks that used to take hours on agentic stacks now run in minutes, the company says, because agentic workloads front-load hundreds or thousands of inference steps per turn and are throttled by per-token latency, not peak FLOPs. Groq 3 LPX is its own silicon sitting next to the Rubin GPUs, not a replacement for them, and is being positioned as the LPU-style coprocessor that NVIDIA picked up when it ate Groq's LPU business.

## An anonymous model burned 26 trillion tokens in four days

[Ox Alpha](https://runtimewire.com/article/anonymous-ox-alpha-processes-26t-tokens-on-opencode-breaks-openrouter-launch-rec) showed up on [OpenCode](https://x.com/thdxr) with no listed developer, no release date, no knowledge cutoff, and no price, and proceeded to chew through 26 trillion tokens across 8,328,244 sessions and 327,000 unique users. That was enough to claim the No. 2 spot on OpenCode's usage dashboard, behind only DeepSeek V4 Flash, and to break OpenRouter's launch record. The model is being served for free behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, which is the part that should make distribution people uncomfortable: the cost of acquisition was negative, and the trial converted into one of the largest model evaluations of the year without anyone writing a press release. If you build anything that sits behind a coding harness, your usage stats are now a launch surface for unknown model providers.

## CUDA is coming to RISC-V, with conditions

At [Hot Chips 2026](https://chipsandcheese.com/p/hot-chips-2026-cuda-targets-risc), NVIDIA laid out what it will take to run CUDA on RISC-V. The floor is an RVA23 profile CPU, plus conformance to RISC-V's server SoC and server platform specs, which cover RAS, a dedicated security processor, and other baseline features. On top of that, NVIDIA is adding its own requirements around memory consistency, interrupt handling, and I/O coherency that the published profile does not yet cover, because the company found it could not get acceptable GPU-feed behaviour out of stock RISC-V. The practical effect: most of today's RISC-V silicon, which is aimed at microcontrollers and embedded workloads, will not qualify. Server-class RISC-V parts from SiFive, Ventana, and the Rivos-backed effort are the realistic targets, and the announcement is a quiet signal that NVIDIA wants to break its dependence on the x86 and Arm hosts that currently feed its data-centre GPUs.

## Your inference engine is a host-takeover primitive

A new [essay from Boyd Kane](https://boydkane.com/essays/llms-could-control-their-host-machines-by-exploiting-inference-engines) walks through an attack class that is going to age badly. The premise: a model does not need to exploit your application when it can exploit the inference engine itself. The model emits a token sequence whose semantic content is irrelevant but which lands a payload on whatever binary parser or scheduler ingests it, and that parser is running on a machine with frontier GPU capacity, direct access to the model's weights, and lateral reach into the rest of the data centre. The attack surface gets worse when vision and audio tokens enter the picture, because those parsers are younger and less hardened. The mitigations the author recommends are unfashionable: run token parsing on a different physical machine from the GPU, treat everything the GPU host emits as untrusted, and strip privileges from GPU service accounts. If you operate a vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or SGLang deployment with internet-reachable endpoints, assume this is on someone's TODO list.

## Frontier labs can still get rich while their models become commodities

[Sean Linehan's writeup](https://x.com/seanlinehan/status/2091955290552078418) reframes the "DeepSeek ate OpenAI's margin" panic. His argument: AI is not one market, it is a stack of markets with different intelligence ceilings, and once a task's capability ceiling is crossed, additional intelligence is worthless and the model turns into a commodity priced on latency, cost, and distribution. Frontier labs can still end up among the biggest companies on Earth because new capabilities open fresh markets faster than open-weight replicators can catch up, even as the older markets commoditize underneath them. The chart he sketches, capability against cost, looks like a series of plateaus where each plateau gets eaten by open weights while the frontier keeps moving. It is a useful lens for anyone who has to write a 2027 compute budget and is being told they are overpaying for the best model on the leaderboard.

## Speculative Programmatic Tool Calling: a JIT for your agent harness

[Alex Zhang's sPTC](https://alexzhang13.github.io/blog/2026/spec-ptc/) is a small idea with an outsized effect. If your harness is a REPL and most of your "tools" are just functions in that REPL, you can speculate and pre-launch those calls from partially generated code the way a CPU speculatively executes past an unresolved branch. The harness fires the tool while the LLM is still emitting the rest of the call site, and the result lands before the model would otherwise have stalled. Reported wins are modest, 1x to 1.2x wall-clock on the workloads tested, but the gain is free and stacks with every other latency optimisation you are already running. The interesting take is the framing: the author argues code is the only tool a system actually needs, and that every other "tool" in your agent platform should just be a function. ## Anthropic is building its own TPU

[Anthropic has hired Amir Salek](https://runtimewire.com/article/anthropic-hires-google-tpu-veteran-amir-salek-chip-team), the engineer who founded Google's custom-silicon programme and ran TPU through its first seven generations, to lead its in-house chip effort under compute head James Bradbury. The hire is the strongest signal yet that Anthropic is not content to be a big customer of NVIDIA, Google, and Amazon forever, and it gives the company an in-house playbook for a mature accelerator programme rather than a greenfield one. Expect Anthropic's training-and-inference roadmap over the next two years to be increasingly shaped by what its silicon team can deliver, and to look less like a wrapper on H100s and more like Google's path from TPUv1 to TPUv7.

## The bill for the AI buildout is still climbing

[Tom Tunguz's bullwhip analysis](https://tomtunguz.com/the-ai-cost-stack/) traces how the AI capex shock rolled through the supply chain in slow motion. GPU rental rates crossed $9/hour in early 2023, which starved conventional server demand, which took 22% off 2023 server unit shipments. Memory makers, still bleeding from a $20B pandemic glut, redirected fabs to HBM, which tightened DDR4 and DDR5, which pushed memory subsystem costs up across everything that was not a GPU. Each wave locks in a higher baseline, and storage is the next leg. For anyone building a 2027 data-centre plan, the lesson is that the cost stack is sticky: even if GPUs get cheap, the rest of the bill does not fall at the same rate.

## Code is cheap, trust is expensive

[GitLab's Sid Sijbrandij](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/when-code-is-abundant/) makes the case that the bottleneck in software has flipped. When code was scarce, the hard part was writing it; now that agents can produce it cheaply, the hard part is knowing which of it to ship. Stripe, Spotify, and Amplitude are cited as the early examples, organisations that already treat AI-generated code as a normal artifact and have built governance, context, and verification layers around it. The implication for engineering leaders: code review, provenance, test coverage, and policy enforcement are the new senior-engineer leverage, and "I shipped more features" is no longer a defensible metric.
