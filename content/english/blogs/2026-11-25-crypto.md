The Ethereum Foundation just said artificial intelligence is a more urgent threat to crypto than quantum computers, and it is tearing out elliptic-curve cryptography to prove it. The plan touches every validator, every wallet, and every blob on the network, and the EF calls the whole thing harder than the Merge. Meanwhile the CFTC quietly decided that bitcoin, ether, and payment stablecoins can now count toward the cash reserves derivatives platforms must hold, which is the kind of regulatory inch that changes what exchanges can actually do with their balance sheets.

Start with the existential one, because it rewires how you should think about every signature on Ethereum.

The [Ethereum Foundation's first PQAI post](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/oqwEdBB0cevcB1NAN9K6TQ/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtY3J5cHRvfGV0aGVyZXVtcy1oYXNoLWJhc2VkLW92ZXJoYXVsLWlzLWhhcmRlci10aGFuLXRoZS1tZXJnZXx8aHR0cHM6Ly9ibG9nLmV0aGVyZXVtLm9yZy8yMDI2LzExLzIzL3BxYWktMD91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20) is blunt about the threat model: AI models find bugs in production software faster than humans can review them, which makes the current elliptic-curve stack a liability. After more than a year of evaluating signature schemes, the EF is going all-in on hash-based cryptography in three places: validators sign with hash-based keys for consensus, wallets get a migration path to hash-based signatures, and blobs switch to hash-based commitments.

This is not a patch. BLS tooling, pre-quantum contracts, wallets, multisigs, and staking pools all need changes. The EF has doubled its protocol security team, and it frames the upgrade as harder than the Merge, which replaced Ethereum's entire consensus engine in 2022 with no downtime. Justin Drake floated December 2028 as a target at Devcon, with 2029 as the EF's internal deadline and a timeline review in January. Ethlabs pulled its Quick Slots proposal out of Hegotá to free up engineering time. If you run validators or maintain wallet infrastructure, the migration path is the thing to watch, because hash-based keys are not a drop-in replacement for what you have deployed today.

The [CFTC's Division of Market Oversight and Division of Clearing and Risk issued a staff advisory](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/tEYQ9mTnn0pw0S3qgf7OyA/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtY3J5cHRvfGNmdGMtbGV0cy1jcnlwdG8tY291bnQtdG93YXJkLWZpbmFuY2lhbC1yZXNvdXJjZXN8fGh0dHBzOi8vd3d3LmNmdGMuZ292L1ByZXNzUm9vbS9QcmVzc1JlbGVhc2VzLzkzMzEtMjY_dXRtX3NvdXJjZT1icmllZi10ZWNoLW5ld3MuY29t) explaining how swap execution facilities, designated contract markets, and derivatives clearing organizations can use crypto to meet financial resources requirements under Regulations 37.1301, 38.1101(a), and 39.11(a)(2). Bitcoin, ether, and payment stablecoins may count toward the requirement to hold financial resources covering at least 12 months of operating costs, subject to haircuts.

The catch: they do not count toward the separate liquidity requirement, which still demands cash or highly liquid securities. So crypto can't fully replace traditional capital reserves. Prior CFTC rules were silent on whether crypto counted toward the operating cost threshold, leaving registered entities guessing how to treat native asset holdings on their balance sheets. This advisory is staff views only, creates no new rules, and carries no enforceable rights, but it is the kind of guidance that lets a clearinghouse hold a chunk of its runway in BTC instead of treasuries.

[Stellar's Bell, Protocol 30, is live on mainnet](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/ZBuH4TBcXH32CEgkxeE1Ww/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjUtY3J5cHRvfHN0ZWxsYXJzLWJlbGwtYnJpbmdzLXNtYXJ0LWFjY291bnRzLWFuZC1xdWFudHVtLXNhZmUtc2lnbmF0dXJlc3x8aHR0cHM6Ly9zdGVsbGFyLm9yZy9ibG9nL2RldmVsb3BlcnMvaW50cm9kdWNpbmctYmVsbC1wcm90b2NvbC0zMC1vbi1zdGVsbGFyP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) following the validator vote on November 23. CAP-84 adds muxed contract addresses, a new "W" format carrying a 64-bit ID, so one smart account can hold funds for many customers in an omnibus model instead of one contract per customer. CAP-87 lets smart contracts verify NIST's ML-DSA post-quantum signatures natively inside Stellar Core. CAP-88 moves ledger close times to a millisecond clock, removing the one-second floor so future upgrades can push block times below the current 5 seconds.

Network transaction signatures stay Ed25519, and the W address reuses muxed-account machinery but is a new address type developers must handle. Indexers or explorers that decode raw ledger data must update their XDR definitions because the ledger header format changes. If you run Stellar infrastructure, that last part is not optional.
