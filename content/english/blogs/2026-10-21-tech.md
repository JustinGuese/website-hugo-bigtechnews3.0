Internet infrastructure teams are scrubbing certificate bundles after [eight intermediate certificates belonging to Indian CA eMudhra](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/6eKyTd-vmk_ZBBWdnHRKLA/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTAtMjEtdGVjaHxlaWdodC1pbnRlcm1lZGlhdGUtdGxzLWNlcnRpZmljYXRlcy1sZWFrZWQtd2Vhay1rZXlzLWF0LWVtdWRocmF8fGh0dHBzOi8vYXJzdGVjaG5pY2EuY29tL3NlY3VyaXR5LzIwMjYvMTAvdGhlLWludGVybmV0LWdvZXMtb24taGlnaC1hbGVydC1hZnRlci1kaXNjb3Zlcnktb2YtOC1jb21wcm9taXNlZC10bHMtY2VydHMvP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) were discovered to have catastrophically weak private keys. The breach was proven publicly when a researcher signed an arbitrary verification message using one of eMudhra’s exposed production signing keys, validating that unauthorized parties could issue trusted TLS certificates for arbitrary hostnames.

Major desktop browsers, mobile operating systems, and root stores pushed revocation blocks for the eight intermediates via CRLite and OCSP stapling. The remaining operational headache sits in headless Linux containers, embedded IoT devices, base Docker images, and local trust stores (such as `/etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt`) that do not dynamically evaluate intermediate revocation chains. SREs should grep legacy base images for eMudhra roots and force rebuilds.
