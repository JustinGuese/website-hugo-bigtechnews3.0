OpenAI has a new chip, and the part that matters isn't the name. [Jalapeño](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/openai-details-jalape%C3%B1o-ai-chip-with-700w-tdp/), the company's first Broadcom-built AI accelerator, is rated at 700W but held at or below 550W in tested inference workloads. That gap between the nameplate number and what the silicon actually pulls under real prompts is the whole story. Inference is the electricity bill, and OpenAI is publicly arguing it can serve more tokens per joule than the H100 or B200 racks everyone else is buying.

Jalapeño is also the first concrete hint of what OpenAI's multi-year Broadcom deal actually produces. The chip is inference-optimised, sits on OpenAI's own networking stack, and is being talked about in the same breath as Google's TPU and Amazon's Trainium. For anyone running GPU fleets, the question is no longer "when does OpenAI build its own silicon" but "how many of my H100-hours does it replace, and at what cost."

## GPT-5.6 Terra and Luna hit AWS GovCloud

AWS has put [OpenAI's GPT-5.6 family on Bedrock inside GovCloud (US-West and US-East)](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/08/openai-gpt-terra-luna-govcloud/), with Terra pitched at "GPT-5.5-level performance at half the cost" and Luna positioned as the cheap-and-fast tier. Both come with million-token context windows and prompt caching that AWS quotes at a 90% discount for repeated context.

The interesting part is the venue. GovCloud is where federal contractors, defence-adjacent SaaS vendors, and regulated industries that can't touch commercial regions are forced to live. Until now, those workloads have mostly defaulted to Anthropic or open weights on Bedrock because OpenAI's frontier models simply weren't available there. Terra and Luna being generally available in GovCloud means OpenAI is now a real option for FedRAMP-style deployments, not just a consumer-API curiosity.

For architects, the cost claim is the thing to pressure-test. "Half the cost of GPT-5.5" is a model-card number, not a workload number, and Bedrock's metering tends to make prompt caching economics look better in slides than in production logs.

## Cisco swallows Supermicro into its NVIDIA AI Factory

Cisco is adding [Supermicro's liquid- and air-cooled high-density servers](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4213820/cisco-bulks-up-its-ai-infrastructure-portfolio-with-supermicros-liquid-cooled-servers.html) to its Secure AI Factory with NVIDIA. The package already includes Cisco UCS, Silicon One and Spectrum-X switches, AI Defense, Isovalent's eBPF security, Splunk, and the full NVIDIA AI Enterprise software stack. Supermicro slots in as the compute layer for customers who need denser GPU packaging than Cisco's own UCS chassis offer.

This is the AI Factory playbook maturing. Cisco sells the network, the security, the management plane, and now a choice of compute vendors, all validated end-to-end with NVIDIA's stack. Buyers get a single throat to choke; Cisco gets to stay in the deal even when the actual GPU boxes are Supermicro. The targets are neoclouds, sovereign AI builds, and the usual parade of enterprises that want NVIDIA parts but don't want to integrate them themselves.

For SREs, the practical question is whether a mixed-vendor reference architecture actually holds up under support contracts, or whether "validated end-to-end" turns into finger-pointing the first time a firmware update breaks something.

## McKinsey: enterprise AI is finally finding ROI

McKinsey's [latest read on enterprise AI](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/25/mckinsey-says-enterprise-ai-is-finally-on-the-road-to-roi/5292388) argues the conversation has shifted. Investment is still climbing, measurable earnings impact is still rare, but the deployments that are paying off share a pattern: they redesigned the workflow rather than bolting a chatbot onto an existing one. McKinsey's framing is the usual consultancy cadence, but the underlying point matters — the gap between "we have a Copilot licence" and "we make more money" is closing only for teams willing to rip out the old process.

For anyone whose CFO is asking hard questions, this is the version of the McKinsey chart to print out: AI spend is rising, ROI is concentrated in a minority of programmes, and the differentiator is operational, not model-shaped.

## "SaaS is dead" is the wrong diagnosis

A widely circulated essay argues [SaaS isn't dying, but standardised SaaS is](https://aicoding.leaflet.pub/3mtu6c5wsvc2n). Cheap AI-assisted software generation makes generic CRUD applications a commodity, which guts the seat-based pricing that funded the last fifteen years of SaaS. Gartner is cited estimating up to $234 billion of enterprise application spend could be exposed to "agentic arbitrage" by 2030, roughly 20% of the market. The author's read is that vendors will survive by selling the things AI can't easily fake: authoritative data, compliance posture, integration surfaces, operational accountability.

The Klarna-Replaced-Salesforce example gets walked back a bit in the piece itself; the more useful frame for engineers is that "the CRM" and "the data under the CRM" are different products, and only one of them is collapsing into a prompt.

## Perplexity and NVIDIA ship a local agent

Perplexity and NVIDIA [launched Portable Computer](https://venturebeat.com/infrastructure/perplexity-partners-with-nvidia-to-launch-portable-computer-a-fully-local-ai-agent-with-zero-token-costs), an agent that runs locally on DGX Spark and RTX-powered Linux machines, bills nothing for tokens spent on-device, and only falls back to cloud models when a task exceeds local capability. The pitch is straightforward: agentic workloads eat tokens for breakfast, and the only way to make the unit economics work is to stop paying a hyperscaler for every tool call.

This is the "local first, cloud optional" model that Ollama and LM Studio have been building toward from the hobbyist side, now arriving with a vendor name attached. For platform teams, the interesting question is governance: a fleet of RTX boxes running agents in your office is great until one of them starts emailing customers on its own.

## Oracle Exadata on AWS: real wins, real gotchas

Oracle's [Exadata Database Service is now generally available inside AWS datacenters](https://www.theregister.com/databases/2026/08/24/oracle-exadata-databaseaws-offers-advantages-but-trade-offs-remain/5291731). Licensing specialists quoted by The Register say it can dramatically reduce processor-license requirements for Oracle-constrained shops, but the compute and storage premiums are steep, capacity scaling is coarser than native AWS databases, and Oracle's sales positioning tends to oversell the savings.

For DBA-adjacent engineers, the calculus is: if your database estate is 80% Oracle and you're paying per-processor licences that scale with every EC2 instance, the licence savings may swamp the AWS markup. If you're a single application on a single instance, this is a worse deal than RDS or Aurora.

## Supabase gives Claude an enterprise-grade auth path

Supabase has shipped [enterprise-managed auth for its MCP server](https://supabase.com/blog/enterprise-managed-auth-for-the-supabase-mcp-server), built with Anthropic and Okta. Until now, anyone connecting Claude to a Supabase project had to approve their own OAuth consent, and only org owners could even initiate the connection. Now an admin authorises the connector once, access flows through the company's IdP, and each employee's Claude session inherits their existing Supabase role and project scopes.

This is the unsexy part of agentic AI actually working inside a real company. An IT team can now turn Supabase access in Claude on for the engineering org, off for the marketing org, and audit both from Okta, without anyone re-doing OAuth by hand.

## Google lets Gemini write your cloud migration business case

Google is adding [Gemini-powered "Quick Assessments" to its Cloud Migration Center](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4213538/google-adds-ai-powered-assessments-to-migration-center-to-speed-up-cloud-migration-planning.html), promising near-instant TCO modelling and automated service mapping. Analysts quoted in the piece caution that this speeds up the slide deck, not the technical discovery — the business case is the easy part, and the actual migration still needs deep architectural validation.

For migration programme leads, treat the output as a draft, not a plan, and budget the human validation step accordingly.

## Open-weight models are quietly winning

The [Vercel AI Gateway data](https://www.constellationr.com/research/blog/open-weight-models-are-gaining-ground-enterprise-ai) tells a striking story: open-weight models were 11% of Gateway token volume in April 2026, 29% in June, and hit 62% on August 22. Roughly one in eight enterprise customers was already running an open-weight model in production by June, and the mix is no longer just "developers kicking the tyres." Cost is the obvious driver, but the piece also flags suitability for agentic workloads and the growth of managed inference providers (Together, Fireworks, the hyperscalers' own endpoints) as structural reasons open weights are now a durable part of enterprise AI rather than a passing experiment.

For anyone architecting AI platforms, "we should support open weights" has moved from roadmap item to table stakes, and the procurement conversation is increasingly about which managed endpoints to expose, not whether to expose any at all.

## IETF 126: IPv6, DNS, and the AI Internet

[IETF 126 set a new attendance record](https://blog.apnic.net/2026/08/25/ai-ipv6-and-the-future-internet-at-ietf-126/) with 1,230 on-site and 672 remote participants, and the throughline across the IPv6, DNS, and AI sessions was that large-scale AI deployments will need both. China's telecom operators presented DNS-Based Address Mapping Records for translating IPv4 to IPv6-only networks, a quietly important piece if your AI cluster is going to talk to a world that is still mostly IPv4.

## Apple's new Macs push local AI inference again

Apple's [new Mac Studio and Mac mini](https://arstechnica.com/apple/2026/08/with-new-mac-studio-and-mac-mini-apple-leans-hard-into-local-ai-inference/) double down on running capable AI models directly on the device. The pitch is the same one Apple's been making since Apple Silicon: enough unified memory and enough neural-engine throughput to keep serious inference off the cloud. Whether the silicon actually matches the marketing is the perennial Apple question, but the strategic direction is unambiguous — local inference is now a first-class target for Mac hardware, not a footnote.
