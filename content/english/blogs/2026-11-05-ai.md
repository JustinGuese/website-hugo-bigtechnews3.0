OpenAI's image model now ships faster than most teams can finish a design review. [Images 2.6](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/BMS2xlPwf0iI77BNgpd71g/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMDUtYWl8fHxodHRwczovL3d3dy50ZXN0aW5nY2F0YWxvZy5jb20vb3BlbmFpLWxhdW5jaGVzLWltYWdlcy0yLTYtd2l0aC1naWZzLWFuZC1pbWFnZS1yZWZlcmVuY2UvP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) is rolling out globally across ChatGPT and Codex on desktop, mobile and web, on every subscription tier, generating images 50% faster than the previous version. OpenAI says its products now produce 4 billion images a week.

The headline feature is motion: users can turn characters, mascots, product shots, sketches and storyboards into short GIF loops, then adjust playback speed, edit individual frames and share them. The quieter feature is consistency - keeping a character, product or brand detail stable across successive editing rounds, which is the part that actually decides whether this is a toy or a production tool.

For developers there are two new API models. GPT-Image-2.6 Flare targets speed and professional layouts like posters and product imagery, and can return an image in as little as six seconds at low quality. Sunburst is the detail model, aimed at medium and high quality settings where edits have to hold.

The interesting part isn't the classifier score, it's the output format. Pangram returns a heatmap that localizes exactly where AI content appears inside an image - including the case of a real photograph with a synthetic element composited into it, which is the scenario that breaks whole-image "is this AI?" checks. Pangram says the model is robust to edits, compression and low resolutions, and ships a Chrome extension plus API support for both images and video. A Flash 1.0 variant sits alongside the flagship.

If you run any kind of upload pipeline - marketplace listings, KYC documents, newsroom submissions - a per-pixel verdict with an API is a different integration than a single confidence float.

OpenAI built an [autonomous pipeline for discovering, exploiting and patching vulnerabilities](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/TOf-LbNyolrGZwRQMclkrQ/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMDUtYWl8b3BlbmFpcy1waXBlbGluZS1mb3VuZC1hbmQtcGF0Y2hlZC05NDgtbGludXgtcHJpdmlsZWdlLWVzY2FsYXRpb25zfHxodHRwczovL29wZW5haS5jb20vaW5kZXgvYS10aG91c2FuZC1mZXdlci1wYXRocy10by1yb290Lz91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20), and in a single run its cybersecurity model validated and generated patches for 948 Linux local privilege escalation flaws.

LPE bugs are the boring, high-value class: a process running as an unprivileged user finds a way to become root, and every container, CI runner and multi-tenant host is exposed. Finding them is expensive because each one needs a working exploit to prove it's real, which is exactly the step most automated scanners skip. OpenAI's claim is that its model both validated the bugs and produced the patches.

What the post does not say is how many patches were merged upstream, or how many survived maintainer review. Treat 948 as the size of the candidate set that passed automated validation, not as 948 fixes in your kernel.

Axiom released a [Lean formalization of the entire Stacks Project](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/8orD1AStc4REhntS53juFQ/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMDUtYWl8dGhlLXN0YWNrcy1wcm9qZWN0LWlzLW5vdy0xNi1taWxsaW9uLWxpbmVzLW9mLWxlYW58fGh0dHBzOi8vYXhpb21tYXRoLmFpL3Jlc2VhcmNoL3RoZS1zdGFja3MtcHJvamVjdC1mb3JtYWxpemVkLz91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20), the roughly 7,500-page open-source reference underpinning modern algebraic geometry. The result spans about 16 million lines of code and Axiom calls it the largest integrated, reusable library of formalized mathematics ever built.

Two details make it more than a stunt. Permanent Stacks tags link every Lean declaration back to the canonical text, so a proof assistant user can trace a machine-checked theorem to the paragraph a mathematician already cites. And because it's one importable library, researchers and AI agents can pull its definitions and theorems directly into new formal proofs instead of re-deriving them.

The Stacks Project started with Columbia's Johan de Jong in 2005 and grew through hundreds of contributors. Turning that into something a proof checker accepts is a different scale of effort from formalizing a single famous theorem.

<p class="small mb-4"><a href="https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/q2Vy8YrHDD9a7kKdUIk9Gg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMDUtYWl8YS1wYXBlci1hcmd1ZXMteW91LWRvbnQtbmVlZC1sb25nLWNvbnRleHR8fGh0dHBzOi8vYXJ4aXYub3JnL2Ficy8yNjExLjA1MzA2P3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Source: arxiv.org ↗</a></p>

A [paper submitted to arXiv](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/q2Vy8YrHDD9a7kKdUIk9Gg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMDUtYWl8YS1wYXBlci1hcmd1ZXMteW91LWRvbnQtbmVlZC1sb25nLWNvbnRleHR8fGh0dHBzOi8vYXJ4aXYub3JnL2Ficy8yNjExLjA1MzA2P3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) measures what your bill already tells you: later tokens cost more to generate than earlier ones, by as much as 7x on frontier open-weight models. The authors show that with frequent compaction, models lose little to no accuracy across long-horizon and long-input tasks when kept on short contexts - and that performance doesn't collapse on long-horizon software engineering even when the model takes at most one action per context. They sketch "semantic state space models" from that result and question whether long-context models are needed at all.

Worth reading against the Kimi release above. The industry is shipping million-token windows and publishing evidence that compaction gets you most of the way there for a fraction of the cost.
