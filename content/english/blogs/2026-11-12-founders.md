<h2 id="the-b2a-economy-agents-are-the-new-customers">The B2A economy: agents are the new customers</h2>

The shift from B2C to B2A - business-to-agent - is about distribution mechanics, not philosophy. Retailers once blocked bots outright; now they compete for their business. The a16z piece argues that companies treating agents as first-class customers will grow, while those blocking them get left behind.

For engineers, this changes what "optimization" means. Human-facing SEO is about clicks, dwell time, and conversion funnels. Agent-facing optimization is about structured data, clean APIs, predictable response formats, and being the thing an agent can reliably call. If agents are writing most production code and buying software on behalf of humans, then your API docs are your marketing site, and your rate limits are your sales pitch. The teams that figure out how to make their product the one an agent reaches for first are the ones that win the next decade of distribution.

<h2 id="who-captures-the-margin-in-ai-apps-win-power-loses">Who captures the margin in AI: apps win, power loses</h2>

Tom Tunguz broke down the AI value chain into five layers and normalized everything to dollars per watt per year. The gross profit pool splits like this: 3% to power, 5% to data center operators, 28% to neoclouds (before GPU depreciation), 24% to inference vendors, and 40% to AI applications.

The interesting part is what's testing those margins. Compute prices are rising - Anthropic's burst compute runs at $13/GPU-hour, and CoreWeave's new contracts are signing at $20-25/w/yr. That's pricing power, but it also means the 28% neocloud margin is under pressure from depreciation. Meanwhile, application margins vary wildly depending on whether the AI actually drives ROI for the customer.

The consolidation play is already visible: CoreWeave's acquisitions show vertical integration is how you absorb price swings. For anyone building on top of these layers, the takeaway is that your cost structure is someone else's pricing decision. If you're an AI application, your gross margin depends on how much of that 40% you can defend against the neoclouds moving up-stack.

<h2 id="saas-renewals-317-price-hikes-and-the-49-negotiation-discount">SaaS renewals: 317% price hikes and the 49% negotiation discount</h2>

Cledara's 2026 benchmarks show SaaS renewal increases averaging 8-12% annually, with aggressive vendors pushing 15-25%. Since 2022, published list prices have risen from about 5% (Salesforce, HubSpot) to 317% for Canva Teams' old five-person plan. Most vendors auto-renew by default and require 15-60 days' notice to cancel.

The number that actually matters: negotiating 90 days before renewal saves 49% on average, versus 19% when you start 30-90 days out. That's a 30-point swing for the price of calendar discipline. For anyone running software budgets, this is the argument for a renewal calendar that flags every contract at the 120-day mark. The vendors know this - that's why they auto-renew and bury cancellation windows in legalese. The engineering answer is to treat contract management like infrastructure: automate the reminders, standardize the process, and never let a renewal sneak up on you.

Google's [Gemini Decisions API](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/bEDc9xgAJ-cK9wvb5fmWZA/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMTItZm91bmRlcnN8Z2VtaW5pLWRlY2lzaW9ucy1hcGktMTAwMC1xdWVzdGlvbnMtb25lLXBhc3MtMjUwbXN8fGh0dHBzOi8vYmxvZy5nb29nbGUvaW5ub3ZhdGlvbi1hbmQtYWkvdGVjaG5vbG9neS9kZXZlbG9wZXJzLXRvb2xzL2dlbWluaS1kZWNpc2lvbnMtYXBpLz91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20) is a new endpoint for closed-set decisions powered by the Gemini Decision 1 model. It processes multimodal inputs - text, images, audio, video, PDFs - up to 256K tokens with up to 1,000 questions, scoring options in a single forward pass. End-to-end latency is 150-250ms, at $0.10 per 1M input tokens and zero output tokens billed.

That's nearly 5x cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with the Interactions API. For routing, policy checks, and grading tasks, this is the first API that makes "ask the model 1,000 questions" a viable production pattern. The confidence scores matter too - you can set thresholds and fall back to human review when the model isn't sure. For anyone building decision systems, this is worth a spike: the cost curve just moved.

<h2 id="grok-bot-10-from-personal-assistant-to-team-tool">Grok Bot 1.0: from personal assistant to team tool</h2>

Grok Bot 1.0, announced November 2, adds Team Bots for publishing and sharing, Slack and Microsoft Teams support, meetings on Google Meet, Zoom, and Microsoft Teams, shared connectors, and reviewed learnings. It's now a team assistant rather than a personal one, with draft-based Team Bots, review queues for learnings, and app-specific shared connectors.

The 1.0 version is on Team, Enterprise, and Ultra plans, with meetings included. For engineers, the interesting bit is the review queue for learnings - that's the governance mechanism that keeps a team-wide AI from learning bad habits. Without it, a shared bot accumulates everyone's mistakes. With it, you get a knowledge base that improves under human review.

<h2 id="ros-refounding-from-zombie-to-3b-run-rate">Ro&#x27;s refounding: from zombie to $3B+ run rate</h2>

Ro was a "zombie company" in 2021: $270M run rate, 9% YoY growth. The 2022 reset cut more than $50 million in direct-to-consumer revenue because the unit economics were upside down. By 2026, Ro is at a $3B+ run rate with over 100% CAGR for three years - nearly 10x growth with fewer people (406 vs 596 peak in May 2022), and ARR per employee up more than 10-fold. It now serves more than 1% of the US population and is the leading GLP-1 provider.

The operational leverage is the story. Growth came from focus, not headcount. For founders, the lesson is that cutting revenue to fix unit economics can be the fastest path to real scale - if the underlying demand is there.
