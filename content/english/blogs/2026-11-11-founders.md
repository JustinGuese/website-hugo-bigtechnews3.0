The pitch is no longer that AI will augment your company. It's that AI will *be* the company. A new essay from The General Intelligence Company argues that within a year there will be millions of profitable autonomous companies - organizations where every management decision is made by agents, with humans only at the very bottom of the hierarchy for specific tasks. That's a reversal from the author's position six months ago, when he argued human oversight was still necessary.

Whether or not you buy the timeline, the essay crystallizes what's actually changing in how software and organizations get architected. The coordination layer - collective purpose, management structure, internal communication, state - is becoming a software problem. That matters even if you think "millions" is fantasy, because the infrastructure being built to get there is real and shipping today.

The AI-native services model targets money that software never touched. Companies like [Crosby](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/W3Zxolr4tc9PwwvzOjc8xg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMTEtZm91bmRlcnN8dGhlLTQ2LXRyaWxsaW9uLXNlcnZpY2VzLW1hcmtldC1pcy10aGUtbmV3LXNhYXMtdGFtfHxodHRwczovL3d3dy5mb2N1c2VkY2hhb3MuY28vcC9pbnNpZGUtdGhlLWFpLW5hdGl2ZS1zZXJ2aWNlcy1idXNpbmVzcz91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20), an AI law firm, charge $250 - $1,000 per contract review. Harper, an AI insurance brokerage, handles 1,000+ customers a month versus 20 - 30 for a human team. The prize is the $4.6T enterprises spend on salaries and services, not the $200B SaaS market.

The hard part isn't getting off the ground - it's scaling without proportionally more people. Revenue comes immediately, but genuine product-market fit is unproven. The model only works if delivery quality holds as volume grows, and that's a distributed systems problem wearing a business-model costume. If you're building one of these, the question isn't whether the first 100 customers are happy; it's whether customer 10,000 gets the same output as customer 1.

The math from Euclid's Verticalist Summit is brutal and simple: divide each closed deal's annual contract value by the human hours spent winning it, including sales engineering and legal. If that number falls as you add reps, fix pricing and packaging before hiring more. [The panel's examples](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/oq2_Sfha8NkCQueY-XbXew/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMTEtZm91bmRlcnN8eW91cmUtaGlyaW5nLXRvby1tYW55LXJlcHN8fGh0dHBzOi8vaW5zaWdodHMuZXVjbGlkLnZjL3AveW91cmUtaGlyaW5nLXRvby1tYW55LXJlcHMtdmVydGljYWxpc3Qtc3VtbWl0LTIwMjY_dXRtX3NvdXJjZT1icmllZi10ZWNoLW5ld3MuY29t) include $15,000 - $20,000 annual contracts closing in six to seven days with a couple of calls and no solutions engineer.

The standout case is GC AI's Cecilia Ziniti, who sold the first $16M with herself and three reps - closing an $80,000 deal in 21 days because she didn't know it was supposed to be impossible. The lesson isn't that sales is dead; it's that the unit economics of human-led sales have changed when AI does the discovery and the first-draft work.

Palantir made the FDE famous; Anthropic is industrializing it. In October, Anthropic committed [$100M to train 10,000 forward deployed engineers](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/QtMggn_WFmLoLhg5U6OXvw/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMTEtZm91bmRlcnN8dGhlLWZvcndhcmQtZGVwbG95ZWQtZW5naW5lZXItaXMtYS1ndG0tZnVuY3Rpb24tbm93fHxodHRwczovL3guY29tL2l0c2FsZXh2YWNjYS9zdGF0dXMvMjEyMDEzODU2MTk5MTQwNTY0Mj91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20) by end of 2027. The role sits with sales, maps manual work, builds workflows in the client's CRM, trains reps, and hands over. Success is scored on manual hours saved, team usage, workflows live, and self-sufficiency.

This is the model for selling AI-driven GTM systems: the engineer is the product's first power user and its best demo. For engineers, it means the boundary between "writing code" and "closing deals" is dissolving. The five-stage process - sit, map, build, train, hand over - is a repeatable playbook, and the $100M bet says Anthropic believes it's the distribution channel that matters.

The narrative that AI startups run lean sales teams doesn't survive contact with [Pave's data from 10,000+ companies](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/0KVc4LKq1obLFzu9il6PyA/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMTEtZm91bmRlcnN8dGhlLXNhbGVzLXRlYW0tbXl0aC1jcnVuY2hlZHx8aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuc2Fhc3RyLmNvbS9haS1zdGFydHVwcy1tYXktaGF2ZS1mZXdlci1zYWxlcy1yZXBzLWJ1dC1ub3QtYXMtYS1wZXJjZW50LW9mLXRoZS10ZWFtLWhhcy10aGUtcGxheWJvb2stcmVhbGx5LWNoYW5nZWQtYWxsLXRoYXQtbXVjaC8_dXRtX3NvdXJjZT1icmllZi10ZWNoLW5ld3MuY29t). Sales is 18.4% of headcount at AI hypergrowth companies versus 20.7% in the rest of tech - a gap of just 2.3 points. The real divergence is elsewhere: customer support is 1.8% versus 4.4%, while engineering is 47% versus 33.9%. ICONIQ's data showing 43% fewer GTM FTEs at $10M - $25M ARR is because AI-forward companies are smaller overall, not because sales is being cut.

The takeaway for org design: AI is reshaping engineering and support roles far more than sales. The sales playbook hasn't changed as much as the hype suggests, and large deals still need humans to navigate procurement.
