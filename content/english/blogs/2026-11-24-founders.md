AI agents are becoming the new sales funnel, and most stores are losing without knowing it. a16z and The Prompting Company ran 11,307 controlled agent shopping tests across Grok Bot, Muse, Instinct, and Dots, plus shopping requests on ChatGPT, Gemini, and Perplexity. The headline: agents converge on defaults. If one agent picked a product, another had a 73% chance of picking the same one. That means the fight isn't for the best product - it's for the default.

The other number that should scare e-commerce teams: better return terms won 86% of equal-price comparisons. And mandatory account signups? Every single agent stopped cold. If your checkout requires a new account, you are invisible to the agent economy. Here's what else is moving for founders, engineers, and the people selling to both.

The [a16z study](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/g2Qaq-tERymsMnb5zbumjQ/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjQtZm91bmRlcnN8dGhlLWIyYS1lY29ub215LWFnZW50cy1waWNrLWRlZmF1bHRzLW5vdC13aW5uZXJzfHxodHRwczovL3d3dy5hMTZ6Lm5ld3MvcC93ZWxjb21lLXRvLXRoZS1iMmEtZWNvbm9teS1ob3ctYWdlbnRzP3V0bV9zb3VyY2U9YnJpZWYtdGVjaC1uZXdzLmNvbQ) is the most concrete look yet at how AI agents actually shop. They don't browse like humans. Muse pulls a maximum of 50 items from its own catalog and browses roughly three product pages before deciding. That's the entire consideration set.

The 73% overlap rate means the agent ecosystem has a winner-take-most dynamic: once one model's training data or catalog ranking favors a product, the others follow. For sellers, that changes the optimization target. Price and reviews still matter, but they matter as inputs to becoming a default pick, not as differentiators. The study also found agents finish guest checkout forms most of the time - so keep guest checkout open, make return terms machine-readable, and target specific use cases where you can flip the recommendation.

Since 1994, eight waves of counter-positioning have each forced incumbents to cannibalize their own revenue to compete. Red Hat monetized open source, hit $1B revenue in 2012, and sold to IBM for $34B. Salesforce built SaaS in 1999. Amazon introduced usage-based pricing in 2006, cutting into Sun Microsystems' server sales.

The pattern: a new model wins because the leader can't copy it without destroying their existing pricing. The current wave - open-source tools like Postiz taking on Buffer - follows the same playbook. For anyone pricing software today, the question isn't whether SaaS is dead. It's which wave you're riding and whether the incumbent can afford to follow you.

[PostHog's advice](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/trX7UPVFo4Ych15kToXIRg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjQtZm91bmRlcnN8c3RvcC1hc2tpbmctdXNlcnMtd2hhdC10aGV5LXdhbnR8fGh0dHBzOi8vbmV3c2xldHRlci5wb3N0aG9nLmNvbS9wL3VzZXItaW50ZXJ2aWV3cz91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20), based on thousands of hours of user interviews: users tell you what they think they want, not what they need. The companion piece from [Jan Miksovsky](https://ecommerce-api.datafortress.cloud/r/brieftechnews/OKkFoOScCbBvXSkwNXbQDg/djF8d2VifDIwMjYtMTEtMjQtZm91bmRlcnN8c3RvcC1hc2tpbmctdXNlcnMtd2hhdC10aGV5LXdhbnR8fGh0dHBzOi8vamFuLm1pa3NvdnNreS5jb20vcG9zdHMvMjAyNi8xMS0yMy1vYnNlcnZpbmctdXNlcnMuaHRtbD91dG1fc291cmNlPWJyaWVmLXRlY2gtbmV3cy5jb20) makes the case for watching users directly. It's rarely done outside video games, but watching one user struggle with a Web Origami tutorial led to a complete redesign. Go to their environment, let them use their own device, and clarify you're testing the product, not them.
